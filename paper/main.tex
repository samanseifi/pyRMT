\title{A semi-implicit log-conformation Reference Map Technique for incompressible
viscoelastic fluid--structure interaction}

\author[bu]{Saman Seifi\corref{cor}}\ead{samansei@bu.edu}
\cortext[cor]{Corresponding author}
\address[bu]{Department of Mechanical Engineering, Boston University,
Boston, MA 02215, USA}



\begin{abstract}
We present a fully Eulerian, one-fluid Reference Map Technique (RMT) for incompressible
fluid--structure interaction (FSI) that couples a finite-strain elastic or viscoelastic
solid to a Newtonian fluid on a single staggered grid with one pressure solve. Two
ingredients make the method both accurate and fast. First, the reference map and the
stress-carrying elastic strain are transported by a low-diffusion bicubic semi-Lagrangian
scheme that needs no level-set reinitialization; the elastic strain is advanced through
its matrix logarithm, so it stays positive-definite by construction and the relaxing
stress is kept separate from the folding-prone reference map, with the neo-Hookean elastic
solid recovered as the long-relaxation-time limit. Second, the coupled system is advanced by an
\emph{implicit--explicit} (IMEX) integrator that treats the fluid viscosity by a direct
sine/cosine-transform solve---exact even at no-slip and moving-lid walls, so the implicit step
costs no more than an explicit one---relaxes the elastic stress exactly, and couples the elastic
force by an energy-conserving trapezoidal rule that conserves the discrete energy of the linear
elastic wave to round-off (a standing-wave test; in the nonlinear coupled setting the explicit
force contributes an $O(\dt)$ drift over a fixed horizon). By
lifting the viscous, elastic-wave and advective step-size limits at no per-step cost it reaches a
target accuracy in fewer, larger steps---a several-fold wall-clock reduction that grows with
resolution. The solver is validated against analytic Maxwell solutions, the Jain disc-in-vortex
convergence study, and the Sugiyama lid-driven cavity, for both elastic and viscoelastic solids.
No reported run uses level-set reinitialization; throughout, the staggered discretization keeps
the velocity divergence-free to machine precision.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Fluid--structure interaction (FSI) problems in which a soft solid undergoes large
deformation inside a flow---valves, swimmers, capsules, gels and polymeric
particles---are numerically demanding because the solid and fluid share a moving
interface across which stress and velocity must remain continuous. Many of these solids are not
purely elastic but \emph{viscoelastic}: cells, capsules, gels, tissues and polymeric particles
store elastic energy yet also relax it over a characteristic time, so their response depends on
the ratio of that relaxation time to the flow time scale. Capturing this dependence is central
to problems ranging from cell and particle sorting in microfluidics to the rheology of
deformable-particle suspensions \cite{Sugiyama2011,Rosti2018prf}, and it is precisely what a
purely elastic solver cannot represent. The dominant
strategies treat the two phases with different kinematics. Arbitrary
Lagrangian--Eulerian (ALE) methods deform a body-fitted mesh with the interface
\cite{Donea1982,Hu2001},  which is accurate but requires remeshing once the
deformation becomes severe. Immersed and fictitious-domain methods keep the fluid
grid fixed and represent the solid through forcing or Lagrange multipliers
\cite{Peskin2002,Zhang2004}, avoiding remeshing at the cost of interface sharpness.
Partitioned couplings that iterate between separate fluid and solid solvers are
modular but suffer the added-mass instability when the densities are comparable, so
that strong coupling or careful relaxation is required for stability
\cite{Causin2005,Hou2012}. These difficulties have motivated a third route: fully
\emph{Eulerian}, one-fluid formulations that discretize solid and fluid on a single
fixed grid, sharing one velocity field and one pressure solve
\cite{Sugiyama2011,Ii2012,Cottet2008,Wick2013}. The interface is captured rather than
tracked, the added-mass coupling is resolved implicitly inside the momentum
balance, and no remeshing is ever needed; the price is that the finite-strain solid
response, naturally posed on a material reference configuration, must be reconstructed on the
fixed Eulerian grid.

The \emph{reference map technique} (RMT) supplies exactly this reconstruction. It
transports the inverse motion---the reference map $\xiv(\xv,t)$ carrying a current
point back to its material label---as an Eulerian field, from which the deformation
gradient $\Ften=(\grad\xiv)^{-1}$ and hence the elastic stress follow pointwise
\cite{Kamrin2012,Valkov2015}. Since its introduction the RMT has grown into a robust
incompressible FSI solver with level-set interface capture and stress blending
\cite{Rycroft2020}, and into a conservative, non-dissipative finite-difference
formulation \cite{Jain2019}; it belongs to the same Eulerian-solid lineage as the
full-Eulerian schemes of Sugiyama \emph{et al.}\ \cite{Sugiyama2011,Sugiyama2010} and
the earlier continuum description of viscoelastic particles by Liu and Walkington
\cite{Liu2001}. The appeal is a single set of conservation laws on one staggered
grid; the open questions concern \emph{what} constitutive branch the solid carries and
\emph{how} the coupled system is advanced in time.

Both questions are consequential. On the constitutive side, the established RMT
formulations carry a purely \emph{hyperelastic} solid \cite{Kamrin2012,Rycroft2020,Jain2019}.
A hyperelastic body sheared without bound stores unbounded energy, and its Eulerian
carrier---the reference map, or equivalently the elastic strain---folds under sustained
shear, so that reinitialization or filtering is used to treat the symptom. A \emph{relaxing}
stress helps, but it is important to state what it does and does not do: relaxation bounds
the stored elastic strain and makes the stress insensitive to a folding reference
map---the stress is then carried by the bounded, always-positive-definite $\be$ rather than
by $\Ften=(\grad\xiv)^{-1}$---but it does not prevent the map itself from folding, and a
more fluid-like (smaller-$\tau$) body in fact stretches and folds sooner. Relaxation thus
relocates the folding difficulty out of the stress; only in the Maxwell-fluid limit, where
the map need not be differentiated at all, is it removed entirely. Transporting a
viscoelastic stress stably is itself a classical difficulty---the high-Weissenberg
number problem, in which a naive update of the conformation tensor loses
positive-definiteness and the computation breaks down \cite{Oldroyd1950,Bird1987}.
The cure is to evolve a matrix logarithm of the conformation tensor, the
log-conformation representation of Fattal and Kupferman \cite{Fattal2004,Fattal2005},
subsequently recast in eigenframe form by Hulsen \emph{et al.}\ \cite{Hulsen2005} and
placed in a broader factorization framework by Balci \emph{et al.}\ \cite{Balci2011},
which guarantees a symmetric positive-definite conformation by construction. Bringing
this representation into the RMT ties the relaxing elastic strain of the solid to the
same stable transport that viscoelastic \emph{fluids} already enjoy, and decouples it
from the folding-prone reference map.

On the time-integration side, the same formulations advance momentum explicitly, so
the step is throttled simultaneously by the elastic-wave speed $c_s=\sqrt{\mu_s/\rho}$,
the viscous diffusion $\dx^2/\nu$, the advective CFL, and---once the solid relaxes---the
relaxation time $\tau$. Stiff solids and high-Weissenberg regimes are then effectively
out of reach. Implicit and monolithic Eulerian FSI does exist, but for
\emph{hyperelastic} solids: Dunne \cite{Dunne2006}, Richter \cite{Richter2013,Richter2017}
and Wick \cite{Wick2013} solve a coupled Newton system for velocity, pressure and a
solid deformation field on a fixed grid, tracking the interface with an ``initial point
set'' that is itself a reference-map-like field. Even there the deformation/interface is
advanced through a separate extension step outside the Jacobian, a treatment that is not
\emph{fully} implicit in the geometry. Sharp-interface implicit FSI for large-deformation
solids has likewise been developed by Kolahdouz \emph{et al.}\ \cite{Kolahdouz2023}, but
through a \emph{partitioned} Dirichlet--Neumann coupling on a separate Lagrangian solid
mesh rather than a monolithic Eulerian solve. Fully Eulerian one-continuum FSI with
finite-strain solids is itself well established in the deformable-particle community: Rosti
and Brandt and co-workers \cite{Rosti2017,Rosti2018jnnfm,Rosti2018prf} carry a viscous
\emph{hyperelastic} (Kelvin--Voigt) solid on a single grid, and Roub\'i\v{c}ek
\cite{Roubicek2022} analyses Eulerian reference-map models of viscoelastic-solid/fluid
interaction. What distinguishes the present method from these is the constitutive branch and
its transport: it is, to our knowledge, the first \emph{reference-map} FSI method to carry a
finite-strain \emph{stress-relaxing} solid with an equilibrium hyperelastic branch---a
standard-linear-solid with a Maxwell branch, hence a genuine relaxation time and Weissenberg
number, in contrast to Kelvin--Voigt formulations that possess neither---advanced by
\emph{log-conformation} transport with semi-implicit integration. (Log-conformation transport of
a conformation tensor is of course standard for Eulerian viscoelastic \emph{fluids}, and has
appeared for capsule/membrane and elastoviscoplastic solids; the novelty here is its use for a
relaxing \emph{solid} with an equilibrium elastic branch inside a reference-map FSI solver.)

This paper develops that method. Its contribution is the numerical formulation itself:
an \emph{implicit--explicit} (IMEX), log-conformation Reference Map Technique for incompressible
viscoelastic FSI on a marker-and-cell (MAC) staggered grid. To our knowledge this is the first
implicit RMT---the technique has been an explicit finite-difference method throughout
\cite{Kamrin2012,Rycroft2020,Jain2019}---and the first to carry a finite-strain, stress-relaxing
viscoelastic solid inside the FSI solver.
The solid stress
is carried by a log-conformation tensor $\psib=\log\be$, positive-definite by
construction and, in the standard-linear-solid form, retaining an equilibrium
hyperelastic branch so the body still returns to shape while $\tau\!\to\!\infty$
recovers the neo-Hookean RMT exactly. The staggered discretization makes the pressure
projection exact---divergence is driven to machine zero, the standard advantage of the MAC
arrangement over a collocated one, which needs no checkerboard or Rhie--Chow fix
\cite{Harlow1965,Chorin1968,Brown2001}. On this spatial method---in which
the reference map and the elastic strain are carried by a low-diffusion \emph{bicubic
semi-Lagrangian} scheme that requires no level-set reinitialization, in contrast to the
conservative finite-difference transport of \cite{Jain2019}---the IMEX scheme makes the method
fast: it treats the fluid viscosity by backward Euler, the relaxation by an exact exponential
update, and the elastic coupling by an energy-conserving trapezoidal rule, lifting the viscous,
elastic-wave and advective step-size limits in a fractional-step setting. The viscous Helmholtz
solve reuses the pressure transforms and, for constant viscosity, is \emph{exact} even at no-slip
and moving-lid walls through a direct sine-transform diagonalization, so the implicit predictor
costs no more than an explicit step and the lifted step converts directly into wall-clock: the
net speedup, benchmarked against an explicit baseline, grows with resolution (supplementary
material). The energy-conserving elastic coupling and the finite-strain constitutive update
follow established practice for stiff nonlinear systems \cite{Ascher1997,Reese1998,Simo1998}.

The method is fully implemented and validated on the coupled elastic and viscoelastic
FSI results. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
\Cref{sec:governing,sec:rmt,sec:constitutive} set out the continuous one-fluid model,
the reference map with its bicubic semi-Lagrangian transport, and the finite-strain
viscoelastic constitutive law;
\cref{sec:discretization,sec:time} give the MAC discretization and the explicit and IMEX
time integrators;
and \cref{sec:verification} presents the numerical examples---constitutive verification
against analytic limits; the disc-in-Taylor--Green benchmark and its viscoelastic (SLS)
extension; the four-roll extension and shear benchmarks; colliding-disc contact in both
regimes; the stiff-solid regime; and the elastic disc in a lid-driven cavity against Sugiyama
\emph{et al.}---with the pure-fluid lid-cavity
(Ghia) validation, the spatial-accuracy check, and the reference-map advection-scheme
comparison collected in the supplementary material.

\section{Governing equations}
\label{sec:governing}

We consider an incompressible medium on a fixed domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^2$
occupied by a Newtonian fluid and one or more finite-strain solid bodies. A single
velocity field $\uv(\xv,t)$ and pressure $p(\xv,t)$ are shared by both phases. Momentum
and incompressibility read
\begin{align}
\rho\left(\partial_t\uv + (\uv\!\cdot\!\grad)\uv\right)
   &= -\grad p + \dive\sig_{\mathrm{d}},
   \label{eq:mom}\\
\dive\uv &= 0,
   \label{eq:incomp}
\end{align}
where $\rho$ is the (piecewise-constant) density. A trace-free penalty contact stress for
solid--solid near-contact is added to the blended solid stress before its divergence is taken;
it is exercised in the colliding-disc example and defined there (\cref{sec:contact}).
The deviatoric stress $\sig_{\mathrm d}$ is
blended between fluid and solid through a smoothed indicator (one-fluid model),
\begin{equation}
\sig_{\mathrm d} = 2\mu_f\,\bm D
   + (1-H)\,\sig_{\!s},
   \qquad
\bm D=\tfrac12\!\left(\grad\uv+\grad\uv^{\!\top}\right),
   \label{eq:blend}
\end{equation}
with $\mu_f$ the fluid viscosity, $\bm D$ the rate-of-strain, and $\sig_{\!s}$ the solid
Cauchy stress (\cref{sec:rmt,sec:constitutive}). The indicator $H\in[0,1]$ is the smoothed
Heaviside of the level set $\phi$, ramping over a transition band of half-width $w_t$
(\cref{fig:rmt-schematic}),
\begin{equation}
H(\phi)=
\begin{cases}
0, & \phi<-w_t,\\[2pt]
\tfrac12\!\left(1+\dfrac{\phi}{w_t}+\dfrac1\pi\sin\dfrac{\pi\phi}{w_t}\right), & |\phi|\le w_t,\\[6pt]
1, & \phi>w_t,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:heaviside}
\end{equation}
so $H\to1$ in the fluid and $H\to0$ in the solid, with $w_t=2\dx$ throughout. The trace of
$\sig_{\!s}$ is carried by the pressure, so only its deviatoric part enters
\cref{eq:mom} up to a redefinition of $p$. The viscous term $2\mu_f\bm D$ is applied
\emph{unblended} across the domain, so the solid carries the fluid viscosity in parallel with
its elastic stress---a weak Kelvin--Voigt element, a modelling choice made here (and by
Sugiyama \emph{et al.}\ \cite{Sugiyama2011}) for a single one-fluid momentum balance; it is a
small effect for the stiff and short-relaxation-time solids considered.

The material occupied by the solid is tracked by the reference map (\cref{sec:rmt}),
from which the level set $\phi$ and the deformation are reconstructed. Boundary
conditions are problem-dependent (no-slip walls with an optional moving lid, free-slip
impermeable walls, or periodic); the pressure boundary condition is chosen consistently
with the velocity boundary condition (\cref{sec:discretization}).

\section{The reference map and the viscoelastic solid}
\label{sec:rmt}

\subsection{Reference map and deformation gradient}
Let $\bm\chi(\Xv,t)$ be the motion carrying a material label $\Xv$ to its current
position $\xv$. The \emph{reference map} is the inverse,
$\xiv(\xv,t)=\bm\chi^{-1}(\xv,t)$, so $\xiv(\xv,0)=\xv$ in the solid. Because $\xiv$
labels material, it is advected passively,
\begin{equation}
\partial_t\xiv + (\uv\!\cdot\!\grad)\xiv = \bm 0 .
\label{eq:xi-advect}
\end{equation}
The deformation gradient $\Ften=\partial\xv/\partial\Xv$ is recovered in the Eulerian
frame from the spatial gradient of the reference map,
\begin{equation}
\Ften = (\grad\xiv)^{-1},
\qquad J=\det\Ften ,
\label{eq:F}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Neo-Hookean solid}
\label{sec:neohookean}
The equilibrium (non-relaxing) branch of the solid is an incompressible neo-Hookean
material,
\begin{equation}
\sig_{\!s}^{\infty} = \mu_s\,\dev\!\left(\Ften\Ften^{\!\top}\right)
   = \mu_s\!\left(\bt - \tfrac12\tr(\bt)\,\It\right)\quad(\text{2-D}),
\label{eq:neohookean}
\end{equation}
with shear modulus $\mu_s$ and elastic wave speed $c_s=\sqrt{\mu_s/\rho}$. Here $\mu_s$
is the small-strain shear modulus in the convention of Rycroft \emph{et al.}\
\cite{Rycroft2020}, in which $\sig_{\!s}\!\propto\!\mu_s\bt$; some authors instead write
$\sig_{\!s}=2\mu_s\bt-P\It$ \cite{Jain2019}, so that \emph{their} $\mu_s$ equals \emph{our}
$\mu_s/2$, which we convert accordingly when reproducing external benchmarks
(\cref{sec:disc-tg}). Throughout, $\mu_s$ denotes the equilibrium (neo-Hookean) modulus and
$G$---introduced in \cref{sec:constitutive}---the modulus of the relaxing Maxwell branch; the
two coincide only when a case sets them equal. In the purely hyperelastic limit ($\tau\to\infty$,
\cref{sec:constitutive}) this is the entire solid stress; the general viscoelastic solid
adds a relaxing branch.

\subsection{Finite-strain viscoelasticity: log-conformation}
\label{sec:constitutive}

\emph{Standard-linear-solid model.}
The solid stress is split into a non-relaxing (equilibrium) neo-Hookean branch
\cref{eq:neohookean} and a relaxing Maxwell branch carried by an elastic Finger tensor
$\be$,
\begin{equation}
\sig_{\!s} = \mu_s\,\dev\!\left(\Ften\Ften^{\!\top}\right)
   + G\,\dev(\be),
\label{eq:sls}
\end{equation}
where $G$ is the viscoelastic modulus. The elastic Finger tensor evolves by the
upper-convected Maxwell (UCM) equation with relaxation time $\tau$,
\begin{equation}
\Dt{\be} - \Lt\be - \be\Lt^{\!\top} = -\frac1\tau\left(\be-\It\right),
\qquad \Lt=\grad\uv,
\label{eq:ucm}
\end{equation}
with $\tfrac{D}{Dt}=\partial_t+(\uv\!\cdot\!\grad)$. Dropping the equilibrium branch
($\mu_s=0$) gives a Maxwell fluid; $\tau\to\infty$ gives a purely elastic solid for
which \cref{eq:ucm} reduces to $\Dt{\be}=\Lt\be+\be\Lt^{\!\top}$, i.e.\ $\be$ tracks
$\Ften\Ften^{\!\top}$ and \cref{eq:sls} returns the neo-Hookean stress. The model
therefore spans, under one framework, elastic solid $\leftrightarrow$ viscoelastic
solid $\leftrightarrow$ viscoelastic fluid.

The model derives from the free energy
\begin{equation}
\Psi=\tfrac{\mu_s}{2}\!\left(\tr\Ften\Ften^{\!\top}-2\right)
      +\tfrac{G}{2}\!\left(\tr\be-2-\ln\det\be\right),
\label{eq:free-energy}
\end{equation}
of which \cref{eq:sls} is the (deviatoric) Cauchy stress, while the relaxation term produces
the non-negative dissipation
\begin{equation}
\mathcal D=\tfrac{G}{2\tau}\,\tr\!\left(\be+\be^{-1}-2\It\right)\ge0.
\label{eq:dissipation}
\end{equation}
Two caveats. \emph{(i) Folding.} Only the relaxing branch is bounded by
relaxation; the equilibrium branch $\mu_s\dev(\Ften\Ften^{\!\top})$ is still built from
$\Ften=(\grad\xiv)^{-1}$ and can fold under unbounded shear, so relaxation eliminates the
folding sensitivity only in the Maxwell-fluid limit and mitigates it otherwise.
\emph{(ii) Incompressibility of $\be$.} The relaxation toward $\It$ does not preserve
$\det\be=1$ (as in Oldroyd-B); the trace is carried by the pressure, and $\det\be$ is
monitored as a diagnostic.

The model has closed-form viscometric limits, used later for verification
(\cref{sec:verification}). For homogeneous simple shear
$\Lt=\dot\gamma\,\bm e_x\!\otimes\!\bm e_y$ the steady solution of \cref{eq:ucm} is
\begin{equation}
b_e^{xy}=\tau\dot\gamma,\quad b_e^{xx}=1+2\tau^2\dot\gamma^2,\quad b_e^{yy}=1,
\qquad
\sigma_{xy}=G\tau\dot\gamma,\quad N_1=2G\tau^2\dot\gamma^2,
\label{eq:shear-limits}
\end{equation}
with $N_1$ the first normal-stress difference. A step shear strain $\gamma$ relaxes as
$b_e^{xy}(t)=\gamma\,e^{-t/\tau}$, and uniform planar extension at rate $\varepsilon$
(Weissenberg number $\mathrm{Wi}=\varepsilon\tau<\tfrac12$) saturates at
\begin{equation}
b_e^{xx}\to\frac{1}{1-2\,\mathrm{Wi}}.
\label{eq:ext-limit}
\end{equation}

\emph{Log-conformation transport.}
Direct transport of $\be$ is either over-diffusive or, if non-dissipative, loses
positive-definiteness and blows up at high Weissenberg number. Following
Fattal--Kupferman \cite{Fattal2004} we evolve
\begin{equation}
\psib=\log\be, \qquad \be=\exp\psib\succ0\ \text{by construction.}
\label{eq:psi}
\end{equation}
Advection of $\psib$ is passive as in \cref{eq:xi-advect}; the local (homogeneous) part
of \cref{eq:ucm} is transformed to $\psib$ as follows, in the eigenframe form of Hulsen
\emph{et al.}\ \cite{Hulsen2005} (equivalent to, but distinct from, the
$\bm\Omega+\bm B+\bm N$ decomposition of \cite{Fattal2004}). In the eigenframe of $\psib$
(equivalently of $\be$), with eigenvalues $\lambda_i=e^{\ell_i}$ of $\be$ and
$\bm M=\bm R^{\!\top}\Lt\bm R$ the velocity gradient rotated into that frame, the local
evolution of $\psib$ is
\begin{equation}
\dot{\psib}\big|_{\mathrm{eig}}
   = \begin{bmatrix} 2M_{11} + \tfrac1\tau(e^{-\ell_1}-1) & \Omega_{12}\\[2pt]
       \Omega_{12} & 2M_{22} + \tfrac1\tau(e^{-\ell_2}-1)\end{bmatrix},
\quad
\Omega_{12}=\frac{\ell_2-\ell_1}{\lambda_2-\lambda_1}\left(\lambda_2 M_{12}+\lambda_1 M_{21}\right),
\label{eq:logconf-rhs}
\end{equation}
rotated back by $\bm R(\cdot)\bm R^{\!\top}$; the diagonal carries stretching and
relaxation, the off-diagonal the eigenvector rotation. In the degenerate limit
$\ell_1\!\to\!\ell_2$ the prefactor $(\ell_2-\ell_1)/(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)\to\lambda_1^{-1}
=e^{-\ell_1}$ while $\lambda_2 M_{12}+\lambda_1 M_{21}\to\lambda_1(M_{12}+M_{21})$, so
$\Omega_{12}\to M_{12}+M_{21}$, recovering $\dot{\psib}=2\,\mathrm{sym}(\Lt)$ at $\be=\It$;
\cref{eq:logconf-rhs} is therefore smooth. In two dimensions the eigendecomposition of the
symmetric $2\times2$ field is available in closed form, so \cref{eq:logconf-rhs} is evaluated
without iterative diagonalization; the code evaluates
$\Omega_{12}=\big[(\ell_2-\ell_1)/(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)\big]\,(\lambda_2 M_{12}+\lambda_1 M_{21})$,
replacing \emph{only the prefactor} by its limit $(\ell_2-\ell_1)/(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)\to
\lambda_1^{-1}=e^{-\ell_1}$ while keeping the factor $\lambda_2 M_{12}+\lambda_1 M_{21}$ when
$|\lambda_1-\lambda_2|<10^{-10}$, so the full product tends to the correct limit
$M_{12}+M_{21}$ in both branches.

\emph{Exact relaxation for stiff $\tau$.}
\label{sec:exact-relax}
The relaxation term $-\tfrac1\tau(\be-\It)$ is stiff for small $\tau$ (high
Weissenberg), forcing $\dt\lesssim\tau$ under explicit integration. We remove this by
Strang-splitting the local update into
$\text{relax}(\dt/2)\!\to\!\text{stretch}(\dt)\!\to\!\text{relax}(\dt/2)$ and
integrating the relaxation substep \emph{analytically}. The relaxation-only equation
$\dot{\be}=-\tfrac1\tau(\be-\It)$ has the exact solution
\begin{equation}
\be \leftarrow \It + (\be-\It)\,e^{-\dt/\tau},
\label{eq:relax-exact}
\end{equation}
which is unconditionally stable for any $\tau$ and positive-definite-preserving, since
\cref{eq:relax-exact} is the convex combination $(1-e^{-\dt/\tau})\It+e^{-\dt/\tau}\be$
of two SPD tensors. The stretch substep uses \cref{eq:logconf-rhs} with $\tau\to\infty$
(explicit, non-stiff, bounded by the velocity-gradient magnitude). For a step strain
this scheme is exact at any $\dt$; the property is verified in \cref{sec:verification}.
\subsection{Level set, extrapolation, reinitialization}
The solid occupies $\{\phi\le0\}$. The level set is reconstructed from the reference map
each step (the material boundary is the image of the initial boundary under $\xiv$), and
the reference map is extrapolated into a narrow band outside the solid so that the gradient in
\cref{eq:F} and the interfacial forces have valid stencils. We use a direct constant-normal
(velocity-)extrapolation of $\xiv$ into the band, following Kamrin \emph{et al.}\
\cite{Kamrin2012} and Rycroft \emph{et al.}\ \cite{Rycroft2020}, rather than solving an
auxiliary PDE (a Hamilton--Jacobi extrapolation equation) to fill it: the direct fill is cheaper
and, because the transported map is already smooth, sufficient. Because the level set is rebuilt
from this smooth reference map at every step and the monotone bicubic transport keeps the map
sharp, the interface \emph{does not require level-set reinitialization}---none of the reported
runs reinitializes.

The reference map \cref{eq:xi-advect} (and the conformation $\psib$) is transported by a
\emph{semi-Lagrangian} backtrace: each grid value is taken as the field interpolated at the
departure point traced back along the flow (RK4 in the trace). Because the departure
interpolation is unconditionally stable, semi-Lagrangian transport carries no advective CFL
restriction---this is what allows the IMEX scheme (\cref{sec:time}) to take steps well
above the explicit advective limit. The interpolation order matters: bilinear interpolation
is only $C^0$ and strongly diffusive, smearing the reference-map gradients (hence $\Ften$ and
the captured interface) over long runs, whereas a \emph{monotone bicubic} interpolant is
$C^1$ and markedly less diffusive. The bicubic form is Catmull--Rom, with the interpolated
value at each departure point clamped to the local minimum and maximum over its $4\times4$
support (a discrete-maximum-principle limiter applied to the transported field $\xiv$ or $\psib$
itself); this suppresses Catmull--Rom overshoot at the reference-map kink without any
physical-quantity clamping. We use the bicubic form throughout; it is what keeps the interface
sharp and the strain accurate on the sensitive parts of the flow (\cref{sec:disc-lid}). The
velocity departure point in the semi-Lagrangian momentum branch (\cref{alg:imex}) uses plain
cubic interpolation (no limiter is needed for the smooth velocity), a distinct stencil from the
limited $(\xiv,\psib)$ transport.
The semi-Lagrangian transport adopted here removes the advective step-size limit while keeping
the map smooth.

\section{Spatial discretization and exact projection}
\label{sec:discretization}
\emph{Staggered (MAC) grid.}
The domain $[0,L_x]\times[0,L_y]$ is divided into $N_x\times N_y$ cells of size
$\dx\times\Delta y$. Pressure and the reference map live at cell centres,
$x$-velocity $u$ on the $x$-faces, and $y$-velocity $v$ on the $y$-faces
(Marker-and-Cell layout \cite{Harlow1965}). The discrete divergence (to centres),
pressure gradient (to faces) and Laplacian ($\dive\grad$) are then \emph{consistent by
construction}, $\mathrm{div}=-\mathrm{grad}^{\!\top}$ and
$\mathrm{Lap}=\mathrm{div}\circ\mathrm{grad}$, so the projection is exact.

\emph{Fractional-step projection.}
Momentum is advanced by a predictor--projector (Chorin) split \cite{Chorin1968}. Given a
predictor velocity $\uv^{*}$, the projection solves a pressure-Poisson problem and
corrects to a divergence-free field,
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2\phi = \frac{\rho}{\dt}\,\dive\uv^{*},
\qquad
\uv^{n+1}=\uv^{*}-\frac{\dt}{\rho}\,\grad\phi,
\qquad
\dive\uv^{n+1}=0\ \text{(to machine precision).}
\label{eq:project}
\end{equation}
On the staggered grid the correction is exact: the projected velocity is
divergence-free to round-off, without the checkerboard mode or Rhie--Chow interpolation
required by a collocated arrangement. This non-incremental (Chorin) form carries an $O(\dt)$
pressure-splitting error \cite{GuermondMinevShen2006}; the explicit $\dt\propto\dx^2$ check of
\cref{sec:disc-tg} confirms this splitting error does not account for the near-first-order
pointwise fields there.

\emph{Direct Poisson solvers.}
The pressure boundary condition is chosen consistently with the velocity boundary
condition. For no-slip/free-slip walls the homogeneous-Neumann Laplacian is diagonalized
by the discrete cosine transform (DCT), with eigenvalues
$\lambda_{k}=-2\!\left(1-\cos(\pi k/N)\right)/\dx^2$; for periodic problems the FFT is
used, $\lambda_{k}=-4\sin^2(\pi k/N)/\dx^2$. Each solve is $O(N\log N)$. These
transforms are reused verbatim by the implicit viscous solve of \cref{sec:time}.

\emph{Blended forcing.}
The solid stress \cref{eq:sls} is blended by $(1-H)$, and its divergence is added to the
face momentum. Contact between solids is applied as a trace-free stress whose
divergence survives the projection \cite{Rycroft2020}.

\section{Time integration}
\label{sec:time}

We advance the coupled system with an \emph{implicit--explicit} (IMEX) scheme that makes the
stiff terms implicit, sharing the exact projection of \cref{sec:discretization} and the bicubic
semi-Lagrangian transport of $(\xiv,\psib)$.

\subsection{Stiff step-size limits}
Advanced fully explicitly---as in prior reference-map solvers---the coupled system is bound by
four step-size limits (three explicit CFLs and a relaxation bound),
\begin{equation}
\dt \le \min\!\Big(
\underbrace{C\,\dx/U}_{\text{advection}},\;
\underbrace{C_\nu\,\dx^2/\nu}_{\text{viscous}},\;
\underbrace{C\,\dx/c_s}_{\text{elastic wave}}\Big),
\qquad \text{and}\quad \dt\lesssim\tau,
\label{eq:cfl}
\end{equation}
with $c_s=\sqrt{\mu_s/\rho}$. The IMEX scheme below removes all four: the viscous, elastic-wave
and advective CFLs by implicit and semi-Lagrangian treatment, and the relaxation limit
$\dt\lesssim\tau$ by the exact substep of \cref{sec:exact-relax}---at no per-step cost. The
quantitative comparison against an explicit baseline (matched-step reproduction and the net
wall-clock speedup) is collected in the supplementary material.

\subsection{The IMEX scheme}
\label{sec:imex}
\label{sec:elastic-stab}%
The IMEX scheme makes each stiff term of \cref{eq:cfl} implicit while leaving the nonlinear
physics (advection, elastic force) explicit.
\emph{Implicit viscosity.} The viscous term is made implicit by
backward Euler, yielding a Helmholtz solve for the predictor,
\begin{equation}
\left(\It-\dt\,\nu\nabla^2\right)\uv^{*}
   = \uv^{n} - \dt\,(\uv\!\cdot\!\grad)\uv^{n} + \dt\,\bm f/\rho .
\label{eq:helmholtz}
\end{equation}
For constant viscosity this operator is diagonalized \emph{exactly} by the same
sine/cosine transforms as the pressure Poisson solve of \cref{sec:discretization}---on
periodic, Neumann, and Dirichlet (no-slip / moving-lid) walls alike---each mode divided by
$1-\dt\,\nu\,\lambda_k>0$. So \cref{eq:helmholtz} is a single direct transform pair per
component, unconditionally stable, with the lid entering as a right-hand-side
inhomogeneity: the wall predictor costs the same as the explicit one, and the lifted step
converts directly into wall-clock. A matrix-free conjugate-gradient solve on the identical
stencil remains available for the variable one-fluid viscosity. \emph{Exact relaxation.} The stiff relaxation of \cref{eq:ucm} is removed by the analytic
Strang substep \cref{eq:relax-exact}, which is $A$-stable in $\tau$ and
positive-definite-preserving (\cref{sec:exact-relax}).

\emph{Elastic-wave coupling.} The nonlinear elastic force
$\bm f^n=\dive\sig_{\!s}(\xiv^n,\be^n)$ is kept explicit (evaluated from the start-of-step
reference map), while the elastic-\emph{wave} operator $c_s^2\nabla^2$, with the frozen constant
coefficient $c_s^2=\mu_s/\rho$, is advanced by the energy-conserving trapezoidal
(implicit-midpoint) rule,
\begin{equation}
\left(\It-\dt\,\nu\nabla^2-\tfrac14\dt^2 c_s^2\,\nabla^2\right)\uv^{*}
   = \uv^{n}-\dt\,(\uv\!\cdot\!\grad)\uv^{n}+\tfrac14\dt^2 c_s^2\,\nabla^2\uv^{n}+\dt\,\bm f^n/\rho,
\label{eq:elastic-stab}
\end{equation}
which keeps the constant-coefficient direct solve of \cref{eq:helmholtz}. The frozen $c_s^2$ is
applied over the whole domain; in the fluid the resulting term is a consistent $O(\dt^2)$
perturbation. The reference map is then re-advected with the implicit-midpoint velocity
$\tfrac12(\uv^n+\uv^{n+1})$, which the wave analysis requires for the coupling to be undamped,
and a \emph{single linear solve} is used---no nonlinear iteration. The force must sit
\emph{inside} the implicit right-hand side, as a von Neumann analysis of the linearized wave
shows (proved below):

\begin{proposition}[Stability of force placement in the one-sided coupling]
\label{prop:vn}
For the linearized elastic wave $\uv_t=c_s^2 d_{xx},\ d_t=\uv$ advanced by the simpler one-sided
implicit coupling ($\uv^{n+1}=\uv^n+\dt\,c_s^2 d^n_{xx}+\dt^2 c_s^2\uv^{n+1}_{xx}$), a Fourier
mode $k$ with $a\equiv\dt^2 c_s^2 k^2\ge0$ has spectral radius $|\lambda|=1/\sqrt{1+a}\le1$:
unconditionally stable, with $O(\dt^2)$ damping that vanishes as $\dt\to0$. Applying the force
\emph{after} the implicit solve is unstable once $a>1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Energy-conserving trapezoidal coupling]
\label{prop:trap}
Advancing the same wave by the trapezoidal (implicit-midpoint) rule gives $|\lambda|=1$ for
every $k$ and every $\dt$: unconditionally stable and \emph{lossless}, exactly conserving the
discrete energy $\tfrac12(\uv^2+c_s^2 d_x^2)$.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\emph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:vn}.} For the linearized wave
$\uv_t=c_s^2 d_{xx}$, $d_t=\uv$ with $\partial_{xx}\to-k^2$ and $a=\dt^2 c_s^2 k^2$, the one-sided
implicit update $(1+a)\uv^{n+1}=\uv^n-\dt c_s^2 k^2 d^n$, $d^{n+1}=d^n+\dt\,\uv^{n+1}$ has
amplification matrix $\bm M=\tfrac{1}{1+a}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1 & -\dt c_s^2 k^2\\ \dt & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
$\det\bm M=1/(1+a)$, $\operatorname{tr}\bm M=2/(1+a)$, so $\lambda=(1\pm i\sqrt a)/(1+a)$ and
$|\lambda|^2=1/(1+a)\le1$. Applying the force \emph{after} the solve keeps $\det=1/(1+a)$ but
raises $\operatorname{tr}$ to $1/(1+a)+1-a$, giving $|\lambda|>1$ once $a>1$. \hfill$\square$

\noindent\emph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:trap}.} The semi-discrete wave
$\dot{\bm w}=\bm A\bm w$, $\bm A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0 & -c_s^2 k^2\\ 1 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
has purely imaginary eigenvalues $\pm i c_s k$; the trapezoidal rule is the Cayley transform
$(\bm I-\tfrac{\dt}{2}\bm A)^{-1}(\bm I+\tfrac{\dt}{2}\bm A)$, whose eigenvalues have unit
modulus (the Cayley transform of a Hamiltonian matrix is symplectic), so the discrete energy
$\tfrac12(\uv^2+c_s^2 d_x^2)$ is exactly conserved. \hfill$\square$

\noindent The solver uses the trapezoidal form for the elastic branch, so a persistently
oscillating solid neither grows nor decays numerically. In the nonlinear FSI setting the
trapezoidal step conserves the discrete energy of the linear elastic-wave operator exactly,
while the explicitly-evaluated nonlinear force adds an $O(\dt^2)$ energy increment per step,
which accumulates to $O(\dt)$ over a fixed horizon---consistent with the first-order
operator-split error. The frozen coefficient is $c_s^2=\mu_s/\rho$; the stabilizer is exercised
only in the stiff pure-neo-Hookean case of \cref{sec:stiff}, where $\mu_s$ is the sole modulus
so the coefficient is exact, while the free-slip coupled runs (\cref{sec:ve-coupled}) are
advanced without it. The standing-shear-wave test (\cref{tab:energy-cons}) confirms the linear
conservation to round-off (drift $\le2\times10^{-14}$ from $0.5\times$ to $8\times$ the elastic
CFL).

\emph{Adaptive advection.} The momentum advection is central differencing (second order, no
numerical diffusion) while \emph{both} the advective CFL and the forward-Euler/central
cell-Reynolds bound are satisfied ($\dt\max|\uv|/\dx\le0.9$ and $\dt\le2\nu/\max|\uv|^2$).
Beyond either bound the unconditionally stable bicubic semi-Lagrangian backtrace is used, so the
IMEX step is limited by neither viscosity, elastic waves, nor advection.

\subsection{The scheme in one step}
\label{sec:schemes}
\Cref{alg:imex} gives one IMEX step, sharing the exact projection and the bicubic
semi-Lagrangian transport of $(\xiv,\psib)$. All coupled FSI results use it (implicit viscosity,
exact relaxation, adaptive advection); the energy-conserving trapezoidal elastic-wave stabilizer
of \cref{sec:imex} is an \emph{optional} large-step device, engaged only when the elastic CFL
binds---the stiff-solid case of \cref{sec:stiff}---while the softer coupled runs take
$c_s^2{=}0$. Because the direct transform solve makes the implicit predictor cost the same as an
explicit one, the lifted step-size limits convert directly into wall-clock (supplementary
material).

\begin{algorithm}[t]\small\caption{\textsc{IMEX} --- one step (implicit viscosity, exact relaxation; optional elastic-wave stabilizer)}\label{alg:imex}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State advect $(\xiv,\psib)$ with $\uv^n$ (monotone bicubic semi-Lagrangian); extrapolate \Comment{$\xiv$ re-transported at the last step if stabilizer on}
\State $\psib\!\leftarrow\!$ Strang local step $\mathrm{relax}(\tfrac{\dt}{2})\!\to\!\mathrm{stretch}(\dt,\,\text{\cref{eq:logconf-rhs}})\!\to\!\mathrm{relax}(\tfrac{\dt}{2})$ (exact relax, $A$-stable $\forall\tau$); $\be=\exp\psib$
\State assemble blended stress $\sig(\xiv,\be)$; explicit force $\bm f^n=\dive\sig$
\If{$\dt\max|\uv^n|/\dx\le0.9$ \textbf{and} $\dt\le2\nu/\max|\uv^n|^2$} $\tilde{\uv}\leftarrow\uv^n-\dt(\uv^n\!\cdot\!\grad)\uv^n$ \Comment{central}
\Else\ \ $\tilde{\uv}\leftarrow$ semi-Lagrangian backtrace of $\uv^n$ (cubic) \Comment{unconditionally stable}
\EndIf
\State solve $\big(\It-\dt\nu\nabla^2-\tfrac14\dt^2c_s^2\nabla^2\big)\uv^{*}=\tilde{\uv}+\tfrac14\dt^2c_s^2\nabla^2\uv^n+\dt\,\bm f^n/\rho$ \Comment{$c_s^2{=}0$ unless stabilizer on; direct DST / FFT}
\State $(\uv^{n+1},p)\leftarrow\textsc{Project}(\uv^{*})$
\State \textbf{stabilizer on} (stiff solid, \cref{sec:stiff}): use $c_s^2{=}\mu_s/\rho$ in the momentum solve and re-transport $\xiv$ with the midpoint $\tfrac12(\uv^n+\uv^{n+1})$ in place of the initial advection; extrapolate \Comment{trapezoidal wave}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Disc in a Taylor--Green vortex}
\label{sec:disc-tg}
A soft disc of radius $r=0.2$ at the centre of a unit box is released in the vortex
$\psi=\psi_0\sin(2\pi x)\sin(2\pi y)$ ($\psi_0=0.05$, $\mu_f=10^{-3}$, $\rho_s=\rho_f=1$, shear
modulus $\mu_s=1.0$); it stretches, elastically retracts and oscillates while the fluid
viscously damps it. We run it at the \emph{elastic limit of the viscoelastic model}
($\tau\to\infty$, so the log-conformation branch is non-relaxing and $\be\to\Ften\Ften^{\!\top}$):
this is the same benchmark that fixes the elastic response, now carried within the unified
constitutive framework rather than by a separate neo-Hookean stress. This is the benchmark of Jain, Kamrin \& Mani \cite{Jain2019}, validated
there against the mixed Eulerian--Lagrangian results of Zhao \emph{et al.}\ and
Robinson-Mosher \emph{et al.}; we compare against all three. (Applying the modulus convention of
\cref{sec:neohookean}, their $\mu_s=0.5$ is a physical shear modulus $1.0$, i.e.\ $\mu_s=1.0$
here; running $\mu_s=0.5$ instead would oscillate ${\sim}1.5\times$ too slowly.)
\Cref{fig:disc-tg-energy} overlays our kinetic- and strain-energy evolution on the three
reference methods; the two energies exchange in anti-phase during the stretch--retract
oscillation. Our \emph{kinetic energy} tracks all three in phase across the
full run, with a first dip of $0.0166$ that sits inside the Jain--Zhao--Mosher ke band
($0.0155$--$0.0168$ across the three methods at that time). The three reference methods already
disagree among themselves on the \emph{strain-energy} amplitude---se peaks of $0.0083$ (the
non-dissipative central scheme), ${\approx}0.0068$ (Zhao) and ${\approx}0.0070$
(Robinson-Mosher). Our se peaks at $0.0067$, matching the Zhao/Robinson-Mosher reference level
($0.0068$--$0.0070$) and just below the less-dissipative $0.0083$, while the oscillation period
($0.40$ vs $0.38$) and peak disc aspect ratio ($1.42$ vs the published $1.40$) agree to within
$5\%$.
The convergence study (\cref{fig:disc-tg-conv}) uses the standard soft-disc parameters
($\mu_s=0.1$, $\mu_f=0.01$, single vortex) of \cref{tab:params}.
The spatial convergence (\cref{fig:disc-tg-conv}, measured against a resolved $N{=}512$
reference) splits cleanly by quantity. The \emph{integrated energies} converge at second
order---the $O(\dx)$ interfacial error largely cancels in the volume integral---so the
kinetic and strain energy track $O(\dx^2)$, matching the second-order energy convergence of
Jain \emph{et al.} The \emph{pointwise fields} (velocity, pressure) are first order: the
mollified Heaviside spreads the stress and pressure jump over a band of width ${\sim}2\dx$,
and the captured interface sits at an $O(\dx)$-different sub-cell position on each grid, so
the near-interface field error is $O(\dx)$ on top of a second-order bulk---intrinsic to the
diffuse interface. It persists when the time step is scaled $\dt\propto\dx^2$ rather than
$\dt\propto\dx$ (finest-grid pressure order $0.79$ vs.\ $0.76$, velocity $1.12$ vs.\ $1.11$), so
the near-first-order fields are not the $O(\dt)$ projection-splitting error
\cite{GuermondMinevShen2006} but the diffuse one-fluid interface. Second order in the
\emph{fields} would require a fully-conservative, discretely-telescoping stencil or a
sharp-interface treatment \cite{Kolahdouz2023}. The order is set by the spatial discretization,
not the time integrator.

\subsection{Elastic disc in a lid-driven cavity (FSI)}
\label{sec:disc-lid}
An \emph{elastic} disc---the model's $\tau\to\infty$ limit, its strain carried by the
log-conformation branch---carried by the cavity flow is compared to the
full-Eulerian result of Sugiyama \emph{et al.}\ \cite{Sugiyama2011} and to Jain
\emph{et al.}\ \cite{Jain2019}. Sugiyama \emph{et al.}\ specify the Mooney--Rivlin
coefficient $c_1=0.05$ with deviatoric stress $\sig_{\!s}'=2c_1\dev\bt$, so the
shear-stress coefficient is $2c_1=0.1$; in our $\sig_{\!s}=\mu_s\dev\bt$ convention this
is $\mu_s=0.1$ (\cref{tab:params}), matching their physical solid.
The centroid trajectory threads the reference all the way to the vortex core. Sugiyama
\emph{et al.}\ publish the trajectory as a set of $(c_x,c_y)$ points without time stamps, so the
distance to their path is measured as the mean nearest-point distance (a time-matched deviation
is not available against a curve with no time); the integrator-to-integrator comparison below,
which shares a clock, is reported as a time-matched deviation. With the monotone bicubic
semi-Lagrangian transport the present ($\tau\to\infty$) trajectory tracks Sugiyama's $1024^2$
result to a mean nearest-point distance of ${\sim}2.5\times10^{-3}$ at $N{=}128$---under $0.5\%$
of the ${\sim}0.6$ trajectory extent---as closely as the conservative reference-map scheme of Jain
\emph{et al.}\ \cite{Jain2019} (${\sim}3\times10^{-3}$) and well within Sugiyama's \emph{own}
$128^2\!\to\!1024^2$ spread (${\sim}1\times10^{-2}$; \cref{fig:disc-centroid} overlays both
references, extracted from the vector artwork of \cite{Jain2019} with a \textsc{PyMuPDF} script
calibrated to the axis tick marks, at a precision of ${\sim}1\times10^{-3}$ set by the plotted
line width---below the deviations quoted here). Carrying the elastic
strain as the log-conformation Finger tensor---rather than reconstructing it by differentiating the
reference map, which winds up as the disc orbits the vortex many times---is what keeps the strain,
and hence the trajectory, accurate on the inner spiral (a direct neo-Hookean run reconstructed from
$\xiv$ tracks Sugiyama slightly less closely there, ${\sim}3.1\times10^{-3}$); bilinear transport,
by contrast, is markedly more diffusive on the inner turns (scheme comparison, supplementary
material). This case is also where we quantify the IMEX speedup---which grows with resolution as the
direct wall solve makes the implicit step free---against an explicit baseline in the supplementary
material.

\subsection{Stiff-solid regime}
\label{sec:stiff}
The introduction motivates implicit integration partly by the stiff-solid regime. A
neo-Hookean disc with $\mu_s=16$ ($c_s=\sqrt{\mu_s/\rho}=4\,U_{\rm lid}$) makes the elastic
wave the binding explicit limit: the explicit scheme diverges once $\dt$ exceeds its elastic
CFL, whereas the energy-conserving trapezoidal coupling (Proposition~\ref{prop:trap}) advances
the same disc stably at $4$--$8\times$ that step with the body intact ($\min J$ above $0.98$).
Implicit integration thus \emph{enables} the stiff regime; because a stiff \emph{elastic} wave
is genuine non-dissipative dynamics, however, a large step that fails to resolve it loses phase
accuracy however stably it is taken---so the accuracy payoff of implicit integration lies in the
\emph{dissipative} stiffnesses (backward-Euler viscosity and, most cleanly, the exact relaxation
substep, accurate to machine precision at \emph{any} $\dt$, \cref{sec:verification}).

\subsection{Viscoelastic (SLS) disc in a Taylor--Green vortex}
\label{sec:ve-coupled}
We now couple the full \emph{standard-linear-solid} of \cref{sec:constitutive} to the flow:
a disc carries an equilibrium neo-Hookean branch $\mu_s\dev(\Ften\Ften^{\!\top})$ and a
relaxing Maxwell branch $G\dev\be$ (from the transported $\psib=\log\be$), here with a
dominant relaxing branch ($\mu_s=0.5$, $G=1.5$), at the extensional centre of the four-cell
Taylor--Green vortex---the disc-in-Taylor--Green setup of Jain \emph{et al.}\ \cite{Jain2019}
(\cref{fig:disc-tg-energy}), where the disc genuinely stretches rather than merely rotating
(\cref{fig:ve-coupled}).

The stored elastic energy shows the defining SLS behaviour: at $\tau\to\infty$ the solid
stores and releases energy in sustained oscillations, whereas shortening $\tau$ dissipates
the relaxing branch---yet the non-relaxing equilibrium branch keeps the body returning toward
its shape, so the energy does not decay to zero as it would for a Maxwell \emph{fluid}. As a
check, the $\tau\to\infty$ curve coincides with an independent neo-Hookean run at the matched
total modulus. The stronger extensional demonstration, where the solid genuinely stretches, is
the four-roll case of \cref{sec:ve-extension}.

\subsection{Elastic vs.\ viscoelastic under sustained extension}
\label{sec:ve-extension}
The clearest demonstration that the log-conformation branch is doing its job is a soft blob
held at the extensional stagnation point of a four-roll mill, where it is stretched
continuously (\cref{fig:ve-extension}). A purely elastic solid ($\tau\to\infty$) stores the
stretch without bound---the elastic Finger tensor grows as $e^{2\dot\varepsilon t}$, the
stress diverges and the computation degrades, as it must for a hyperelastic body under
sustained extension. With a finite relaxation time the upper-convected Maxwell relaxation
balances the stretching and $\be$ \emph{saturates}: the centre stretch $b_e^{xx}$ settles at
the analytic planar-extension plateau $1/(1-2\mathrm{Wi})$ (to within a few percent for
$\mathrm{Wi}=0.125$ and $0.25$), and the blob reaches a steady filament rather than blowing
up. This side-by-side elastic/viscoelastic contrast---impossible for the tumbling
lid-driven and vortex discs, where $\be$ stays bounded for any $\tau$---is the sharp test of
the viscoelastic implementation.

($\tau\to\infty$, top, blue) and viscoelastic ($\mathrm{Wi}=0.25$, bottom, red) solid at
$t=2,3,4$. The elastic blob thins without bound; the viscoelastic one reaches a steady necked

\subsection{Viscoelastic disc in shear: a deformable-particle benchmark}
\label{sec:shear}
To test the coupled viscoelastic \emph{solid} against the phenomenology of deformable
particles in flow, we place a soft disc at the centre of a plane-shear cell (top and bottom
walls translating at $\pm U$, giving a mean shear rate $\dot\gamma=2U/L_y$) and measure its
steady Taylor deformation $D=(a-b)/(a+b)$ and orientation $\theta$ from the second-moment
tensor of the solid, as functions of the elastic capillary number
$\mathrm{Ca}=\mu_f\dot\gamma/G$ (viscous stress over elastic modulus). This is the classic
deformable-particle-in-shear test \cite{Sugiyama2011,Jain2019}, here for a finite-strain
viscoelastic solid carried by the reference map and log-conformation.
For the elastic disc (\cref{fig:shear}a) the small-Ca response follows the universal
small-deformation limit---$D\!\to\!\mathrm{Ca}$ with the particle aligning toward
$\theta\!\to\!45^\circ$ as $\mathrm{Ca}\!\to\!0$---and departs sublinearly at larger Ca as the
particle stiffens geometrically and rotates toward the flow ($\theta$ falling to
${\sim}22^\circ$ at $\mathrm{Ca}=0.4$), reproducing the deformation--orientation behaviour
established for elastic and hyperelastic particles \cite{Sugiyama2011}.
Turning on the relaxing branch (the standard-linear solid, \cref{fig:shear}b) lowers the steady deformation
monotonically with the Weissenberg number $\mathrm{Wi}=\dot\gamma\tau$: under sustained
shear the Maxwell branch accumulates a steady elastic stress $\sim G\tau\dot\gamma$ that
stiffens the particle, and $\mathrm{Wi}\!\to\!0$ recovers the elastic curve. This is the
viscoelastic-solid signature the title promises, absent from a purely elastic solver.
The elastic-branch deformation and orientation reproduce the trend reported by Gao \& Hu
\cite{GaoHu2009} for a two-dimensional elastic particle in shear; for the SLS branch no
published finite-strain viscoelastic-particle $D(\mathrm{Wi})$ curve exists---this is the new
regime---so the monotone decrease is anchored by the steady Maxwell-stress estimate
$\sim G\tau\dot\gamma$ above. The runs use a unit shear cell at $N=96$ (\cref{tab:params}).

viscous/elastic---no surface tension; $D$ is the Taylor deformation parameter). (a) Elastic

\subsection{Contact: colliding discs}
\label{sec:contact}
Solid--solid contact is handled not by a repulsive body force---which the exact projection
would annihilate---but by a trace-free contact \emph{stress} added to the blended solid stress
before its divergence is taken \cite{Rycroft2020}, so the resulting momentum-conserving force
survives the projection. Concretely, where the two smeared interfaces come within an activation
distance $\epsilon$ of touching---measured by a linear contact intensity
$f(\phi)=\tfrac12(1-\phi/\epsilon)$ for $\phi<\epsilon$ and $0$ otherwise---the trace-free stress
\begin{equation}
\bm\tau_{\rm c}=-\eta\,\min(f_a,f_b)\,(G_a+G_b)\Big(\bm n\otimes\bm n-\tfrac12\It\Big),
\qquad \bm n=\frac{\grad(\phi_a-\phi_b)}{|\grad(\phi_a-\phi_b)|},
\label{eq:contact}
\end{equation}
is added to the blended solid stress, with dimensionless strength $\eta$ and activation width
$\epsilon=3\dx$ tied to the mesh, so the contact layer refines with resolution; the runs below
use $\eta=2$. Two soft discs, immersed in the viscous fluid, are launched head-on:
they approach, squeeze the intervening fluid out, deform into contact, and rebound
(\cref{fig:contact-snaps}). \Cref{fig:contact} tracks the centroid gap; the contact stress
prevents interpenetration (the gap dips below $2R$ as the soft discs flatten against each other,
then recovers). At a matched total modulus---so the only difference is relaxation---the elastic
discs rebound more strongly than the viscoelastic (SLS) ones, whose relaxing Maxwell branch
dissipates part of the collision energy. The contact stress enters the momentum solve through its
right-hand side. Because the rebound \emph{is} elastic-wave physics, these runs resolve the
elastic wave directly---the elastic-wave stabilizer of \cref{alg:imex} is left off, as for every
non-stiff solid, and engaged only in the stiff regime of \cref{sec:stiff}---so the collision is
integrated without numerical damping of the bounce.

\emph{Coefficient of restitution.} Sweeping the impact speed, we extract a coefficient of
restitution $e=v_{\rm sep}/v_{\rm app}$ (the centroid separation speed over the approach speed,
from the gap trajectory, using the smooth Heaviside-weighted centroid so the measurement is free
of hard-mask staircase noise) and plot it against the impact Stokes number $\mathrm{St}=\rho V_0
R/\mu_f$ (\cref{fig:restitution}a). This probes the two asymptotic limits of
elastohydrodynamic-collision theory \cite{Davis1986}: a slow impact is strongly damped by the fluid
($e\!\to\!0$ below a critical Stokes number), while a lossless elastic collision conserves kinetic
energy ($e\!\to\!1$ as $\mathrm{St}\!\to\!\infty$, the fluid becoming negligible). The elastic
discs reproduce this behaviour---$e$ rises monotonically with $\mathrm{St}$---and the viscoelastic
(SLS) discs sit below the elastic curve at every speed, the Maxwell branch dissipating part of the
impact. The measured restitution is grid-convergent (\cref{fig:restitution}b): at $N{=}128$ it is
within a few percent of $N{=}160$, and it is only on the resolved grids that the elastic and
viscoelastic curves separate---the physical dissipation difference is a resolved effect, not a
coarse-grid artefact. The comparison is qualitative---the penalty contact stress is not the
lubrication-film physics of the elastohydrodynamic theory, and the diffuse interface limits the
precision---and within the accessible $\mathrm{St}$ window the absolute $e$ stays well below the
inviscid ceiling because the fluid dissipation at these moderate Stokes numbers is genuinely large;
what the test establishes is the trend, the elastic-versus-viscoelastic ordering, and the
grid-independence of the number.

\section{Conclusions and outlook}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented an incompressible, one-fluid Reference Map Technique for viscoelastic
fluid--structure interaction on a single staggered grid. The stress-carrying elastic
strain is transported as a log-conformation tensor, guaranteeing positive-definiteness and
decoupling the relaxing stress from the folding-prone reference map, and the staggered
discretization makes the projection exact. On this spatial method---in which the reference map
and elastic strain are carried by a reinitialization-free bicubic semi-Lagrangian scheme---we
built an \emph{implicit--explicit} (IMEX) time integrator that lifts the viscous, relaxation,
elastic-wave and advective step-size limits, reusing the same sine/cosine transforms as the
pressure projection (an exact direct solve even at no-slip and moving-lid walls), with an
adaptive semi-Lagrangian advection and, for stiff solids, an energy-conserving trapezoidal
elastic-wave stabilizer that conserves the discrete elastic energy to round-off.
Because the implicit predictor costs the same per step as an explicit one, the lifted step
converts directly into wall-clock: benchmarked against an explicit baseline (supplementary
material), the net speedup tracks the step-size gain and \emph{grows} with resolution (up to
$5.6\times$ at $N{=}128$).
We demonstrated the coupled
viscoelastic response on a disc in a Taylor--Green vortex: $\tau\to\infty$ recovers the
elastic dynamics, finite $\tau$ dissipates stored elastic energy, and the elastic Finger
tensor remains positive-definite throughout. In the lid-driven cavity the $\tau\to\infty$
limit reproduces the pure neo-Hookean disc directly (\cref{fig:disc-centroid}).

Relative to the closest prior work, the method differs from explicit hyperelastic RMT
\cite{Kamrin2012,Rycroft2020,Jain2019} in both the viscoelastic constitutive transport
and the implicit time integration, from conformation-tensor viscoelasticity in that the
carrier is a \emph{solid} with an equilibrium branch inside an FSI solver, and from the
GPR unified model \cite{Peshkov2016,Dumbser2016} in being incompressible and implicit
rather than compressible and explicit/semi-implicit.

The method has several limitations. It is two-dimensional; all reported runs use equal density
($\rho_s=\rho_f$), density contrast being available through the variable-coefficient projection
path but not exercised here; the diffuse-interface FSI fields converge below second order; and
near-contact resolution is penalty-based. The dissipative one-sided
elastic coupling (Proposition~\ref{prop:vn}) adds numerical damping at large $\dt$, which the
energy-conserving trapezoidal coupling (Proposition~\ref{prop:trap}) removes; the coupled runs
use the latter.

Between these two elastic couplings---the dissipative one that damps every elastic mode and the
trapezoidal one that damps none---lies a tunable middle ground. A generalized-$\alpha$ coupling
\cite{ChungHulbert1993} sets the amplitude factor of the infinite-frequency elastic mode to a
single parameter $\rho_\infty\in[0,1]$, damping the unresolvable grid-scale elastic noise toward
$|\lambda|=\rho_\infty$ while leaving the resolved deformation essentially undamped ($|\lambda|\to1$
as $c_s k\,\dt\to0$), at second order and one constant-coefficient solve per step. On the linear
elastic wave it is stable and near-energy-conserving well past the explicit elastic-wave CFL with
frequency-selective dissipation; wiring it into the finite-strain FSI coupling is a natural next
step for the stiff-solid regime.

Looking ahead, natural extensions are three dimensions, variable density and true
sedimentation, lubrication-corrected contact \cite{Fai2018}, and multi-mode/nonlinear
(Giesekus, FENE-P) constitutive laws.
